# What makes a story ad work: research notes

Collected for the `story-ad-playbook` skill, November 2026. Each finding lists its source and how much weight it deserves. Sources at the end.

## 1. Story structure predicts liking and sharing

| Finding | Evidence | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Ads with a **complete five-act story** (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, denouement, after Freytag's pyramid) got higher consumer ratings than ads without one; ratings rose with each act present | Quesenberry & Coolsen 2014, *Journal of Marketing Theory and Practice*: 108 Super Bowl ads over two years [1][2] | Peer-reviewed, correlational |
| The same holds online: among **155 viral ad videos**, those with full story development had more shares and views. Only 25% used all five acts and 31% told no story at all; ad length and company size did not explain the effect on shares | Quesenberry & Coolsen 2019, *Journal of Interactive Marketing* 48: 1–16 [3][4]. Quesenberry says 4–5-act videos got more than 4× the shares of 0–3-act ones (interview) [5] | Peer-reviewed, correlational; the 4× figure is from an interview |
| A narrative with a **dramatic arc** (tension that rises, then resolves) raised attention-related cortisol and empathy-related oxytocin in viewers, unlike a flat narrative with the same characters | Paul Zak, "Why Your Brain Loves Good Storytelling" (HBR 2014) and related work [6][7]. The "261% more likely to donate" figure circulates only in secondary sources | Lab studies; the donation figure is unverified |

**Use:** every ad, even 15 seconds, gets all five acts, compressed. Tension must rise before the product resolves it.

## 2. People must step inside the story

| Finding | Evidence | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **Narrative transportation** (being absorbed in a story) changes beliefs and attitudes; it depends on empathy with characters, mental imagery and a suspension of reality | Green & Brock's transportation-imagery model [8][9] | Established theory |
| Narrative processing builds **self-brand connections**: people fit brands into the stories of their own lives | Escalas 2004, *Journal of Consumer Psychology* [8][10] | Peer-reviewed |
| When viewers **do not identify with the character**, a story ad can do worse than a plain factual ad | Reported in Dessart et al. 2018 (citing Escalas) [11] | Peer-reviewed review |
| The transportation effect is **stronger for commercial stories and for user-generated ones** than for non-commercial and professional ones | van Laer, Feiereisen & Visconti meta-analysis, 64 articles [8] | Meta-analysis |

**Use:** a character the audience recognizes itself in, within the first seconds. A native, made-by-a-person feel can help (UGC transports better).

## 3. Where the brand goes

| Finding | Evidence | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **Joy and surprise** keep viewers watching internet video ads; the effect of joy is asymmetric (gains help more than drops hurt) | Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters 2012, *Journal of Marketing Research* 49: 144–159; facial-expression tracking, eye tracking and zapping [12][13] | Peer-reviewed lab study |
| **"Pulsing" the brand**, brief appearances spread through the ad with the same total exposure, cut zapping by about 8–10% compared with one long brand display | Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters, *Marketing Science* (moment-to-moment optimal branding) [12][14] | Peer-reviewed |
| Google's ABCD: **brand early and often, with sound and picture together**; open with attention-grabbing sound and visuals; connect through humor, surprise and intrigue; give direction (a call to action) | Google with Ipsos, Nielsen and Kantar; Google cites up to +30% short-term sales likelihood and +17% long-term brand contribution (Kantar, 11,000 ads) [15][16] | Industry, partly self-reported |
| TikTok's own guidance: **prioritize the hook in the first 6 seconds** using suspense, surprise or another emotion; ads have a hook, a body that shows the product and brand, and a close with a call to action | TikTok Ads help center and Creative Codes [17][18]. A secondary source says 63% of top-CTR TikTok videos show their key message or product in the first 3 seconds; most recall impact happens in the first 6 seconds (internal TikTok analysis via Marketing Brew) [19] | Platform guidance; numbers secondhand |

**The conflict, and the fix.** Story craft wants the reveal late; the evidence wants the brand early and repeated. Resolve it the way the pulsing research suggests: **plant the product inside the story early as an object the story uses** (it is on the shelf, in a hand, in the background), let it recur, make it **cause the turn at the climax**, then tag the brand. The name and logo get their full moment at the reveal, and the product has been on screen all along.

## 4. Emotion builds brands over time

| Finding | Evidence | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Emotional campaigns work better over the long term (via emotional priming) and were more than **twice as efficient** as rational ones; rational messaging wins short-term effects; recommended split about 60% brand building : 40% activation | Binet & Field, *The Long and the Short of It* (IPA 2013), IPA Databank of ~996 campaigns [20][21][22] | Industry databank, widely cited |
| Ads with more **"right-brain" features** (living characters, a story, a sense of place, humor and the like) are more effective: 5% of high-feature ads earned 5 stars vs 0% of low-feature ones; the average UK ad now has 3.9 left-brain vs 2.3 right-brain features | Orlando Wood, *Lemon* (IPA/System1) [23][24] | Industry research; the exact feature list was not verified here |

**Use:** characters, a place, a feeling and a story are not decoration; they are the effective part. Keep the hard sell (price, offer) for the last seconds or a separate cut.

## 5. Story craft tools

- **Story Spine** (Kenn Adams, an improv exercise from the 1990s, made famous as rule 4 of Emma Coats's Pixar list): *Once upon a time… Every day… Until one day… Because of that… Because of that… Until finally… And ever since then…* [25][26]
- **Pixar's 22 "rules"** (Coats 2011, she later preferred "guidelines"): we admire a character for trying more than for succeeding; what is your character good at and comfortable with, so throw the opposite at them; know the ending before the middle; why must you tell this story? [25][27]

## 6. The AI fruit-drama trend (asked about specifically)

- Started late February 2026 with a TikTok of a cheating strawberry; the flagship series *Fruit Love Island* launched 13 March 2026 and reached about 3 million followers and 300 million views within days [28][29][30].
- Analysts credit the **serial format and audience participation**, not the AI, for the growth [31].
- Criticism: many storylines are built on cheating, racism and abuse of female characters; one analysis calls it a case study in algorithmic misogyny [28][32].
- Brand caution from trade press: audiences accept AI content as entertainment but can push back when brands use it to replace human creators or to cut costs; serialized TikTok content is emerging as an ad format [33][34].
- **Not verified:** the specific toothbrush-birthday fruit ad. No reporting was found on it; it is used in the playbook as a hypothetical pattern, not as a cited case.

**Use:** the format lessons (serial episodes, a clear contrast pair, an absurd world with a recognizable emotion, comment-bait choices) transfer well. The cruelty does not: build contrast without punching down, which also protects the brand.
